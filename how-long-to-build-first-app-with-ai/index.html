How Long It Takes to Build Your First App with AI

A realistic timeline for beginners: hours to a first working version, days to something real people can use, and what actually eats your time.

A complete beginner can usually get a first working version of a small project in 1 to 3 hours with an AI tool, and reach something other people can actually use in roughly 10 to 20 hours of real work, typically spread over several sessions across one or two weeks. The surprise is where the time goes: generating the first version is the fastest part of the job, while testing, correcting and publishing take most of the hours.

That gap is why "build an app in 5 minutes" demos feel dishonest once you try it yourself. They are not lying about the 5 minutes. They are only showing the first 5 minutes.

A realistic timeline by project type

These are planning estimates for someone with no programming experience, working alone, using a mainstream AI tool.

  • One-page website (personal page, landing page for an idea): first version in 20–40 minutes; published at a real address in 2–4 hours total.
  • Small task app (add items, mark them done, data that survives a refresh): first version in 30–60 minutes; usable by other people in 8–15 hours total.
  • Spreadsheet-to-report automation (take a table, produce a summary you send every week): first version in 1–2 hours; dependable enough to trust weekly in 5–10 hours total.

If your idea involves user accounts, payments or other people's personal data, do not use these numbers. Those projects add a category of decisions that are not about speed at all, and they deserve their own planning.

Where the hours actually go

For a small task app, a typical split looks like this:

Step Time What happens
Describing what you want 20–40 min Turning a vague idea into a request with concrete screens, actions and rules
First build 10–30 min The AI produces something you can open
Testing and correcting 4–8 h Clicking everything, finding what breaks, describing it, guiding fixes
Publishing 30–60 min Getting it online at an address you can send to someone
Fixing after other people try it 2–4 h The problems you never would have found alone

Notice the shape: the two steps everyone talks about are the two shortest ones. Budget your time around testing, not around generating.

Why fast demos and slow reality both exist

A demo proves the screen can appear. A project proves it keeps working when someone else uses it, on their phone, with their data, doing something you did not anticipate.

There is also evidence that AI assistance does not automatically mean faster work, even for professionals. In a randomized controlled trial, METR found that experienced open-source developers took 19% longer to complete tasks when allowed to use early-2025 AI tools, despite believing they had been sped up. The lesson for a beginner is not "AI is useless." It is that the felt speed and the real speed are different things, so plan with hours, not with the feeling of momentum.

Example: a three-session plan for a task app

Here is a schedule you can copy. Three sessions of about 2 hours, plus a short fourth one.

Session 1 — Decide and describe. Write the request before you open the tool. Use this structure:

Build a simple task app for one person.

Screens: one page, nothing else.
Actions: add a task, mark it done, delete it.
Rules:
- Tasks stay after I close and reopen the page.
- Done tasks move to the bottom and show as grey.
- Empty task text is rejected with a visible message.
Look: plain, readable on a phone.

Do not add accounts, login or any feature I did not list.
Before you build, list in one sentence what you understood.

That last line is the cheapest time-saver in this article. If the AI's one-sentence summary is wrong, you fix it in 30 seconds instead of discovering it 40 minutes later.

Session 2 — Break it on purpose. Add a task with an empty name. Add 50 tasks. Refresh the page. Open it on your phone. Click delete twice quickly. Write down each failure as: what I did, what I expected, what happened. Then feed them to the AI one at a time, never in a batch of six.

Session 3 — Publish and test the real link. Put it online, open the address in a different browser where you are not logged in, and walk through everything again. Things that worked on your machine frequently do not work at the public address, and this is the moment to find out.

Session 4 (30 minutes) — Watch one person use it. Say nothing while they click. Write down every hesitation. Fix the top two.

The five things that slow beginners down the most

  • Vague first requests. "Make a task app" costs you an hour of corrections. Twenty minutes of specifics saves it.
  • Changing the goal mid-build. Adding "and also a calendar" at hour two restarts the testing you already did. Keep a list called "version 2" and put it there.
  • Reporting problems as feelings. "It's broken" produces a guess. "When I click delete on the second task, the third one disappears" produces a fix.
  • Skipping the publish step until the end. Publishing early, even when the project is ugly, surfaces an entire class of problems while they are still cheap to fix.
  • Testing only the happy path. You know how it is supposed to be used, so you never use it wrong. Everyone else will.

How to know you are actually finished

A project is done enough to share when all of these are true:

  • It works at a public address, not only on your computer.
  • Data survives closing and reopening the page.
  • Doing something wrong shows a clear message instead of a blank screen or a crash.
  • It is readable on a phone.
  • Someone who is not you completed the main task without being told how.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take a complete beginner to build their first app with AI? Expect 1 to 3 hours to get a first working version you can click through, and roughly 10 to 20 hours of real work, usually spread over several sessions, before it is good enough for other people to use. The build itself is fast; testing, fixing and publishing are what take time.

Why do AI tools promise an app in five minutes? Because a demo and a usable project are different things. Five minutes gets you a screen that looks right. It does not cover saving data reliably, handling mistakes, publishing at a stable address, or fixing what breaks when someone else opens it.

What takes the most time when building with AI? Testing and correcting. Writing the request and generating the first version are usually the shortest steps. Most beginners spend the bulk of their hours checking what the AI produced, describing problems precisely and guiding fixes.

Do I need to learn to code to finish a project with AI? No, but you do need to read results critically and describe problems clearly. You should be able to say what you expected, what happened instead and where, which is a skill you can practise without writing code yourself.

Is it faster to start with a website, an app or an automation? A one-page website is the fastest first win, usually one sitting. A small task app takes a few sessions. An automation that turns a table into a report sits in between and often delivers the most value because you reuse it every week.
