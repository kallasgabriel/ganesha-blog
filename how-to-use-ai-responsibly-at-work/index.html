How to Use AI Responsibly at Work: A 6-Step Checklist

A practical guide to using AI responsibly at work: a 6-step checklist to verify accuracy, protect private data and know when a human must review.

Using AI responsibly at work means treating every AI output as a draft you are accountable for, not as a finished answer. In practice it comes down to three habits you can build in a week: verify anything factual before you pass it on, keep confidential and personal data out of tools that are not approved for it, and route work to a human reviewer whenever the output affects money, people, legal exposure or safety. Most of the "responsible AI" material online is written for companies writing policies — this guide is written for the person actually sending the email.

What responsible AI use looks like day to day

Forget the frameworks for a moment. At an individual level, responsible use is a small set of decisions you make on each task:

• Is this a good task for AI at all? Drafting, summarising, rephrasing, brainstorming and reformatting are strong uses. Being the source of truth for facts, numbers or policy is not. • What am I about to paste in? Once it is in the prompt, assume it has left your control. • What in this output can I actually check? Names, numbers, dates, quotes, citations and links are claims, not prose. • Who else has to see this before it goes out? The higher the consequence, the earlier a human reads it. • Am I comfortable putting my name on it? If you are using AI to do part of your job, you own the result. Alberta's career service puts it plainly: review everything so thoroughly that you are as satisfied as if you had done the work yourself (alis).

The 6-step checklist to run before you send an AI output

Copy this into your notes app. It takes about two minutes on a normal task.

  1. Scope — Say out loud what the output is for and who reads it. A brainstorm for yourself and a message to a client do not deserve the same scrutiny.
  2. Sanitise the input — Strip names, account numbers, credentials, unreleased figures and anything under NDA before you paste. Use placeholders.
  3. Separate claims from prose — Highlight every fact in the output: numbers, dates, names, quotes, sources, product behaviour, legal or policy statements.
  4. Verify each claim at the source — Open the original document, the vendor's page, or your internal system. Microsoft's own guidance for workers is the same: handle data with caution and always double-check AI-generated results before using them (Microsoft Support).
  5. Decide on human review — Customer-facing, legal, financial, medical, HR, safety or hard to undo? It goes to a person first.
  6. Record what you did — One line is enough: "AI-drafted, figures verified against the Q3 report, reviewed by Ana." This is what protects you three months later.

If you only adopt one step, make it step 4. Almost every embarrassing AI incident at work is a plausible-sounding fact that nobody opened the source for.

How do you actually verify an AI answer?

Three rules that do most of the work:

• Never ask the same chatbot to confirm its own answer. "Are you sure?" is not verification. The model will often agree with you, or reverse a correct answer, because it is optimising for a coherent reply, not for truth. • Go to a primary source you can open. Not a summary of the source — the source. If the AI gives you a link, click it and confirm the page says what the answer claims. • Treat citations as claims. A citation that looks real is the single most common failure mode. Check that the document exists, and that the specific sentence is in it.

A useful habit for non-technical work: ask the model to mark its own uncertainty before you start checking. It will not be perfect, but it tells you where to look first.

A reusable prompt for checking your own work

Paste this after you get a draft. It turns a wall of text into a short list of things to verify.

Review the draft below as a fact-checker, not as an editor.

1. List every factual claim: numbers, dates, names, quotes,
   sources, product behaviour, legal or policy statements.
2. For each one, label it: VERIFIABLE (I can check it in a
   document), ASSUMPTION (you inferred it), or UNSUPPORTED
   (you have no basis for it).
3. For each VERIFIABLE claim, say exactly what document or
   system I should open to confirm it.
4. Flag anything that would need a human specialist —
   legal, financial, medical or HR.
5. Do not rewrite the draft. Output only the list.

Draft:
"""
[paste your draft here]
"""

The output is your to-do list. Anything labelled UNSUPPORTED either gets deleted or gets a source.

Before and after: a real work task

The task: you have to email a client explaining why a delivery slipped by two weeks.

Before (irresponsible, and very common). You paste the internal thread — client name, the contract value, a colleague's comment about a supplier problem — and ask for a polite email. You get something fluent, you skim it, you send it. Three things just went wrong: confidential data left the building, the model invented a "new delivery date of the 14th" that nobody agreed to, and it committed to a discount you cannot offer.

After (two extra minutes). You paste a sanitised version:

Write a short, professional email to a client explaining a
two-week delay on a deliverable.

Context:
- Cause: a supplier dependency, now resolved. Do not name
  the supplier.
- New date: [DATE — I will fill this in]
- Do not offer any discount, credit or compensation.
- Do not invent dates, figures or commitments. If you need
  information I have not given you, leave a [PLACEHOLDER].

Tone: direct, accountable, no over-apologising. Max 150 words.

You get a draft with placeholders instead of inventions. You fill in the real date from the project plan, your manager reads it because it is client-facing and involves a commitment, and it goes out. Same time saved, none of the exposure.

Frequently asked questions

What does it mean to use AI responsibly at work? It means treating AI output as a draft you are accountable for, not as a finished answer. In practice it comes down to three habits: check the accuracy of anything factual before you pass it on, never paste confidential or personal data into a tool that is not approved for it, and send work to a human reviewer whenever the output affects money, people, legal exposure or safety.

How do I check if an AI answer is accurate? Separate the output into claims and opinions, then verify each checkable claim against a primary source you can open yourself, such as the original document, the vendor's own page or your internal system. Do not ask the same chatbot to confirm its own answer, and treat names, numbers, dates, quotes, links and citations as unverified until you have seen them in the source.

What should I never paste into an AI tool? Anything you would not post on a public channel: customer or employee personal data, credentials and API keys, unreleased financials, contracts under NDA, medical or legal records, and source code or documents your organisation classifies as confidential. When you need that context, replace the sensitive parts with placeholders such as [CLIENT] or [AMOUNT] and put the real values back yourself afterwards.

When does an AI output need human review? Any time the output goes to a customer or the public, involves a legal, financial, medical or HR decision, affects someone's rights or employment, or would be expensive to undo. A useful rule: if you would have asked a colleague to read it before AI existed, you still need that colleague to read it now.

Do I have to tell people when I used AI? Follow your organisation's policy first, because disclosure rules vary by company, sector and jurisdiction. Where no policy exists, the safe default is to disclose when AI shaped the substance of the work or when the reader could reasonably assume a human produced it, and to be transparent rather than to guess.
